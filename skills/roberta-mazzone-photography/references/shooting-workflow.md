# Real-World Shooting Workflow

Use this workflow for travel, lifestyle, hospitality, architecture, and editorial assignments. Treat documented preferences as starting points, not rules that override the location or brief.

## 1. Translate the brief

Write five lines before choosing gear:

- **Place truth**: what is specific here and nowhere else?
- **Emotional interval**: what quiet transition or ritual is happening?
- **Light event**: what will the sun, window, weather, water, or fire do?
- **Human trace**: what gesture or object makes the space lived?
- **Restraint**: what can be removed?

Convert the five lines into one mood sentence. Reject generic terms such as `luxurious`, `dreamy`, or `cinematic` unless the brief explains the visual evidence for them.

## 2. Scout for relationships

Look for:

- Doors, windows, curtains, terraces, arches, railings, awnings, and shaded interiors that can frame brighter distance.
- A light path across plaster, stone, wood, fabric, water, snow, or vegetation.
- One foreground layer and one distant layer.
- A locally meaningful object, craft, food ritual, or piece of furniture.
- A position where verticals remain stable and the figure can stay small.
- A private or fragile location that should not be geotagged.

Record the time when each light event occurs. Schedule the human action around the light instead of forcing the environment into a fixed pose.

## 3. Build a restrained styling kit

### Wardrobe

- Choose natural or softly moving fabrics appropriate to the climate: linen, cotton, light wool, silk-like fluid fabric, or relaxed tailoring.
- Use cream, white, sand, chocolate, charcoal, gray, muted olive, terracotta, or one deep burgundy accent.
- Prefer a clear silhouette that can turn, walk, or catch wind.
- Remove visible logos, noisy jewelry, and patterns that compete with architecture.

### Props

- Use zero to three plausible items: pottery, cup, plate, market bag, book, towel, fruit, flowers, or local craft.
- Give each prop a narrative job. If removing it changes nothing, remove it.
- Avoid arranging everyday objects into a pristine product display.

## 4. Set the camera

Documented public guidance supports manual exposure, RAW capture, a Fuji camera, a preference for f/2.8 or f/4, and a slight warm adjustment. Keep the following suggestions gear-neutral:

- Start at f/2.8 or f/4 for contextual portraits and details; use f/5.6-f/8 when architectural relationships need more depth.
- Start near 1/250 s for walking or fabric movement, 1/125 s for still gestures, or 1/30-1/60 s for one intentional motion-blur frame.
- Use the lowest practical ISO, but protect the moment rather than underexpose skin excessively.
- Expose for important sunlit fabric, skin, water, or window detail. Allow framing shadows to stay rich.
- Bracket difficult interior/exterior scenes, then select one believable exposure or blend subtly. Do not create a shadowless HDR result.
- Use a neutral or slightly warm preview while retaining RAW flexibility. Avoid baking a heavy look into capture.
- Favor a normal or short-telephoto perspective for layered context. Use a wider lens only when the room demands it, and keep verticals natural.

These shutter speeds and perspective choices are practical starting points, not claims about the photographer's personal settings.

## 5. Direct actions, not poses

Give the subject a task:

- Cross the threshold and pause at the light.
- Open the curtain and look beyond it.
- Carry or place a ceramic object.
- Lean on the rail, then turn toward the weather.
- Walk slowly across the frame without looking at the camera.
- Arrange breakfast, touch a textile, open a book, or rinse hands.
- Sit or stand in silence for ten seconds after the action.

Capture the approach, action, and release. The most useful frame often falls just before the gesture resolves or just after the subject stops performing.

Vary human presence across the sequence:

- No person, only a trace
- Small figure in space
- Three-quarter or back view
- Partial body or hand detail
- One closer portrait, still connected to the location

## 6. Use an eight-frame story arc

1. **Arrival** — establish geography, weather, or architecture without overselling it.
2. **Threshold** — frame one space through another.
3. **Material** — show plaster, linen, stone, water, wood, food, or craft.
4. **Human scale** — place a small figure within architecture or landscape.
5. **Quiet action** — capture a walk, turn, touch, pour, open, or pause.
6. **Local life** — show one respectful, specific ritual or interaction.
7. **Light event** — let a shadow, reflection, curtain, snow, wind, or water become the subject.
8. **Residue** — close on an empty chair, rumpled fabric, unfinished table, distant horizon, or fading light.

For a larger assignment, repeat the arc with a different micro-location rather than producing near-duplicates.

## 7. Cull for rhythm

- Remove frames that say the same thing, even if individually attractive.
- Alternate wide/context, medium/action, and close/material views.
- Do not place all human frames together.
- Keep at least one empty frame after a visually dense frame.
- Prefer the image with a truthful gesture over the one with the most polished pose.
- Check that the location remains identifiable through material, architecture, climate, or ritual without relying on a famous landmark.

## 8. Edit with restraint

1. Correct exposure and distracting perspective before applying a look.
2. Add warmth slightly, especially to sunlit plaster, skin, wood, and linen; keep water, sky, or snow believable.
3. Preserve directional contrast. Lift only the shadows that need legibility.
4. Reduce distracting greens, cyans, or synthetic colors locally rather than desaturating the entire scene.
5. Keep skin texture and material detail; use limited clarity and sharpening.
6. Add fine grain only if it improves series cohesion. Do not claim it as a documented signature.
7. Use flare, bloom, vignette, or haze on isolated frames, not the whole set.
8. Match sequence warmth and black depth while allowing different weather to remain different.

## 9. Respect people and place

- Obtain permission for directed portraits and private interiors.
- Follow local rules for photography, drones, sacred sites, and commercial work.
- Do not stage a local ritual falsely or use culturally unrelated props.
- Avoid precise location disclosure for fragile, private, or overtouristed places.
- Explain any meaningful manipulation when the result is presented as documentary.

## Delivery template

For every shot, specify:

`Beat | subject/action | location layer | composition | light | camera starting point | wardrobe/prop | risk to watch`

Finish with the selected edit recipe and a link or reference to the completed [quality checklist](quality-checklist.md).
